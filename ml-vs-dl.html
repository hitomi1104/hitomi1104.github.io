Choosing Between Machine Learning and Deep Learning: A Practical Decision Guide

Introduction

One of the most common early mistakes in an AI project is reaching for deep learning because it's newer, when a simpler model would be cheaper, faster, and easier to explain, or the reverse: forcing a classic model onto data it can't handle. This guide gives teams a quick, evidence-based way to make that call before they spend money on data, GPUs, or the wrong vendor.

Description

The guide is a two-page reference with three parts:

  • The core difference: a side-by-side comparison of classic ML and deep learning (who defines features, data needs, compute, explainability, and common algorithms).
  • A six-question decision checklist covering data type, whether features can be named, data volume, explainability, compute budget, and the cost of an error.
  • Two real-world case studies run through the checklist: Apache SpamAssassin, where classic ML fits, and IDx-DR, the first FDA-authorized autonomous AI diagnostic, where deep learning fits.

Objective

I wanted to turn a course comparison of machine learning and deep learning into something a decision-maker can actually use. Managers and project leads often have to approve an AI approach without a technical background. The guide gives them the right questions to ask, and gives engineers a shared vocabulary for explaining their recommendation.

Process

  1. Started from a course report. For AIML-500, I wrote a report comparing a machine learning example (spam detection) with a deep learning example (image recognition), each tied to a real-world application.
  2. Chose verifiable, real examples. I picked SpamAssassin because its scoring and Bayesian classifier are publicly documented, and IDx-DR because its FDA authorization and trial results are published. I checked facts such as trial size, sensitivity, and specificity against the primary sources.
  3. Identified the decision logic. Across both cases, the same factors decided the right approach: data type, whether features can be named, data volume, explainability, compute, and the cost of an error. I turned these into the six-question checklist.
  4. Revised for a new audience. I cut the academic structure, led with a one-sentence rule of thumb, replaced long paragraphs with comparison tables, and kept the whole guide to two pages so it's usable in a meeting.
  5. Tested the checklist on both cases. I confirmed that running each case study through the checklist produced the right verdict, and added a rule of thumb: start with the simplest model that meets the requirement.

Value Proposition

This guide helps teams pick the right AI approach in minutes instead of weeks of trial and error. By asking six concrete questions before building, a team can avoid overspending on deep learning where a simple, explainable model would work, or underinvesting where only deep learning can reach the accuracy the problem demands.

Unique Value

  • Grounded in real deployments, not textbook toy examples: one open-source system in daily use, and one FDA-authorized medical device with published trial results.
  • Built from practice. I've built feature-engineered models such as XGBoost for demand forecasting and worked with unstructured regulatory documents like FDA drug labels, so the trade-offs in this guide are ones I've had to weigh myself.
  • Written for both audiences. It works for a non-technical decision-maker and for an engineer explaining a recommendation, which is the bridge I aim to be on AI projects.

Relevance

Choosing the approach is one of the earliest and most expensive decisions in any AI project. In applied and forward-deployed engineering roles, engineers routinely have to explain to customers why a simpler model is the better choice, or why a harder problem needs deep learning. This guide captures that conversation in a reusable form, and it reflects a principle I follow in regulated work: use the simplest system that meets the requirement, and be able to explain why.
